# Writing a CV That Works

What to weigh when writing or tailoring. Where a specific application asks for something different, the application wins.

## Length
- **Jobs and internships:** one page, especially early in a career. Recruiters scan in about six seconds and often skip a second page.
- **Master's and academic applications:** two pages is normal. Education and research get room.

## Order
- Students, new graduates and every Master's application: **Education first**, then Experience.
- After a few years of work: Experience first.

## Bullets
- Action verb plus a measured result, not a duty. "Cut reporting time from 2 days to 3 hours" beats "responsible for reporting".
- If it cannot be measured, say what changed because of it.
- One bullet, one result. Drop anything that only restates the job title.
- Bold the number or result, so the six-second scan lands on it.

## Skills and certifications
- Certifications go inside Skills rather than in a section of their own.
- Lead with the skills the role screens for.

## Automated screening (ATS)
- One column, standard section titles, real text. The default template already is.
- Use the posting's own words for titles, tools and skills, but only where they are true.
- PDF is fine almost everywhere. Make a .docx only when a form demands one.

## Regional conventions
- A photo and date of birth are expected in a few countries and discouraged in most. Leave them out unless an application asks.
- Nationality and location matter for Master's programmes (fees, visas). Add nationality only when a form asks.
- Europass is the EU standard format. A clean custom CV is accepted wherever Europass is; use Europass only when it is required.

## What makes it memorable
- The combination and the story set a person apart, not any single line. Lead with what is unusual about them.
- Volunteering, clubs and projects belong on a student CV: they show the rest of the person.
- Interests: one line, only if it says something the rest of the page does not.

## Letters
- **Answer the question asked.** A programme's prompt ("why this programme", "describe a challenge") decides the structure, not a template.
- **Open with the reason, not with "I am writing to apply".** The first lines say why this organisation or programme, in terms only this one fits.
- **Two or three results, each with its number,** chosen for what the offer asks for. A letter that lists the CV again wastes its one advantage: it can say why the results matter.
- **Name the organisation's own specifics:** a team, a course, a project, a professor. Generic praise reads as a mass letter.
- **One page or the stated limit,** whichever is shorter. Cut adjectives before results.
- **Same language as the offer,** unless the application says otherwise.
